This should look very familiar to you. That's the average, which I'm calling μ today, for reasons that will become clear later, even though that's usually reserved for its more angelic sibling, the expected value.

If you think about it, you may realize that this is obviously just how you calculate it. It doesn't say much about why you'd want to do that. What's the point? What is its job? What does it do?

You might say that it tells you what size the numbers in your sample typically are. Notice how difficult it is not to say "on average".

Now, I also know that you know that can't be true. Because I know that you know that the median exists, and it does the job of representing the typical better than the average. Either they give you about the same number, or the median gives you a better one.

So what gives? Why do we keep on finding the average if that's not what it's for?

For the longest time, I didn't have an answer to that. Way beyond getting my PhD, even though I was much more intimate with all kinds of properties of both than anyone has any practical reason to be.

Then I read this book: Seven Pillars of Statistical Wisdom, by Stephen Stigler, and my mind was blown. I highly recommend it. It somehow manages to be both profound and beautiful at the same time, which is obviously surprising given the subject.

The first pillar of statistical wisdom is — spoiler alert — the average. Or, actually, it's called Aggregation, but it's the same thing. You'll see in a minute.

The book has this picture.

And this really drove the point home to me, and I hope to you too. This is a woodcarving from the 1600s called Determination of the Lawful Rod.

Now, what is a rod? It's a unit of length equal to 16 feet. You use it to measure land.

The foot is a super convenient unit of length, or it would be, but feet don't come in a standard size. Some are smaller and some are bigger.

So the genius point here is that you get your rod to be equal to 16 average feet by making it equal to the sum of 16 random feet.

The point I'm making here is that this equation and the picture you just saw are the same thing.

And this is the job of the average. It doesn't represent the typical; it represents the total. It's the number that gives you the total when you multiply it by the size of your sample.

But wait, there's more.

When I say that the total and n times the average are equal, I'm saying that there is no difference between them. I'm making no error if I replace one with the other. The difference, or the error, is zero.

Now, if I write this left-hand side in just a slightly different form, I'm saying that if I replace each individual value with the average, the errors cancel each other out. They sum up to zero. In total, I'm making no error.

Individually, yes, possibly even to the extent that I'm overestimating most of them and badly underestimating a few — which is exactly what happens with a skewed distribution. Individual errors, yes. In total, no.

Now, if someone comes to you with this equation and says, "Yo, find me a number that behaves like this", meaning that it makes the errors cancel out, you might think: Hmm, sounds like a pretty magical number. I wonder if that even exists.

But it does. It's your friend, the average.

You might also ask why you'd want to find this number in the first place. Some of you may have realized this was coming, or perhaps this sum of errors looks familiar.

The reason you might want to find the number that makes the sum of errors equal to zero is that it's the number that solves this optimization problem:

Minimize the sum of squared errors.

You've heard of least squares, right?

And now you may realize that I've been going backwards the whole time, working up from the solution to the problem. Like this was a Christopher Nolan movie or something.

But now, remember that I started with the question of what's the point of the average? And now I've apparently arrived at an answer: it's the least squares thing!

But the question then becomes: what's the point of least squares?

You may also remember that I insisted that it was this first rewrite, the one showing that the average gives you the total, that was the real deal. And I still say so.

Least squares is the device that turns the equation "errors should sum up to zero" into an optimization problem.
